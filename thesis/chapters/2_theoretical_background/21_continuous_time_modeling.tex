\section{Continuous-Time System Modeling}
\label{sec:21_continuous_time}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Dynamical Systems with Inputs and Outputs}
\label{sec:211_dynamical_systems}

A model is a mathematical representation of a real system.
It is used to describe the system behavior and to answer questions by analysis or simulation.
The same physical system can be represented by different models.
The choice of model depends on the question of interest and on the required level of detail \cite{astrom_feedback_2008, karnopp_system_2012}.

A dynamical system is a system whose behavior changes over time.
In such systems, the effect of an action does not occur instantaneously.
Instead, the future behavior depends on the current condition of the system and on external influences.
Examples are mechanical motion, electrical circuits, and thermal processes \cite{astrom_feedback_2008}.

Complex systems are  described as compositions of smaller subsystems.
Each subsystem interacts with its environment through inputs and outputs.
Inputs represent external influences acting on it, and outputs represent variables that are provided to other subsystems or to the environment.
This input-output view supports the modular description of larger systems from connected subsystems \cite{karnopp_system_2012}.

In addition to inputs and outputs, a dynamical system is characterized by its state.
The state is the smallest set of variables that, together with the input and the initial condition, determines the future behavior of the system.
State variables represent the current internal state of the system.
The set of all possible states is called the state space.
In physical systems, state variables often represent quantities related to stored energy.
For example, in mechanics, they might be position and velocity, in electrical systems, they might be capacitor voltages and inductor currents, and in thermal systems, they might be temperature ~\cite{astrom_feedback_2008,karnopp_system_2012}.
System parameters such as mass, length, or inertia are not state variables, since they do not evolve dynamically during the simulation.

A common representation of continuous-time dynamical systems is the state-space form
\begin{equation}
    \dot{x}(t) = f(x,u,t), \qquad y(t) = h(x,u,t), \qquad x(t_0) = x_0,
\end{equation}
where $x(t)$ is the state vector, $u(t)$ is the input vector, and $y(t)$ is the output vector, and $x_0$ is the initial state at time $t_0$.
The function $f$ describes the time evolution of the state, while $h$ defines how the outputs are obtained from the state and the inputs.
Together with a given input trajectory $u(t)$, the initial condition $x_0$ uniquely determines the future state evolution.
If the functions do not depend explicitly on time, the system is called time-invariant \cite{astrom_feedback_2008}.
This explicit state-space form is typical for ordinary differential equation models.
More general physical models may lead to implicit or differential-algebraic formulations.

As a running example used throughout this chapter, we consider a rigid pendulum with angular position $\theta$, angular velocity $\omega$, moment of inertia $I$, and an applied torque $\tau$ at the hinge (see Figure~\ref{fig:pendulum_simple}). Let $L$ denote the distance from the hinge to the center of mass.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/2_theoretical_background/pendulum_simple.pdf}
    \caption[Simple pendulum]{Simple pendulum with torque input $\tau$, angle $\theta$, rod length $L$, mass $m$, and moment of inertia $I$.}
    \label{fig:pendulum_simple}
\end{figure}

The angular position $\theta$ and angular velocity $\omega = \dot{\theta}$ are convenient state variables, since together they contain the information required to predict the future motion for a given input torque.
The equation of motion is
\begin{equation}
    I\ddot{\theta} = \tau - m g L \sin\theta.
\end{equation}

With $x = [\theta,\omega]^\top$, $u=\tau$, and the output choice $y=\theta$, the state-space form becomes
\begin{equation}
    \dot{x}_1 = x_2,
    \qquad
    \dot{x}_2 = \frac{1}{I}\bigl(u - m g L \sin(x_1)\bigr),
    \qquad
    y = x_1,
    \qquad
    x(t_0) = \begin{bmatrix}\theta_0 \\ \omega_0\end{bmatrix}.
    \label{eq:pendulum_ode}
\end{equation}
For the chosen output $y=\theta$, the output depends only on the state and not directly on the current input $\tau$.
This structural property is referred to as direct feedthrough and is discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:213_direct_feedthrough}.

\subsection{Ordinary and Differential-Algebraic Equations}
\label{sec:212_ode_dae}

Many continuous-time systems can be described by ordinary differential equations.
In this case, the time derivative of the state is given explicitly as a function of the current state, the input, and time \cite{kunkel_differential-algebraic_2024,css_cellier_kofman}.
A general nonlinear \ac{ODE} can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \dot{x} = f(x,u,t),
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the state vector and $u$ is the input vector.

However, not all continuous-time models are naturally expressed in explicit \ac{ODE} form.
In many physical systems, algebraic constraints are present in addition to the differential equations.
These constraints may arise, for example, from geometric relations, constitutive relations, or conservation laws \cite{css_cellier_kofman, karnopp_system_2012}.
Such models are more naturally written as differential-algebraic equations.

A general \ac{DAE} can be written in implicit form as
\begin{equation}
   0 = G(\dot{x}, x, z, u, t),
\end{equation}
where $x$ collects the differential variables, $z$ collects the algebraic variables, and $u$ denotes the input.
In contrast to an \ac{ODE}, the derivatives are not given explicitly.
Instead, differential and algebraic relations are stated together \cite{kunkel_differential-algebraic_2024}.

A common semi-explicit form of a DAE is
\begin{align}
    \dot{x} &= f(x,z,u,t),\label{eq:semi_DAE_derivative} \\
    y &= h(x, z, u, t),\label{eq:semi_DAE_output} \\
    0 &= g(x,z,u,t)\label{eq:semi_DAE_algebraic},
\end{align}
where $x$ contains the differential state and $z$ contains the algebraic variables.
The equation~\eqref{eq:semi_DAE_derivative} describes the time evolution of the states, equation~\eqref{eq:semi_DAE_output} defines the outputs, and equation~\eqref{eq:semi_DAE_algebraic} imposes algebraic constraints that must hold at every time instant.
This representation is useful because it separates dynamic behavior from purely algebraic relations \cite{kunkel_differential-algebraic_2024,css_cellier_kofman}.

The pendulum introduced in Section~\ref{sec:211_dynamical_systems} provides a simple example.
In generalized coordinates, its dynamics are described by the \ac{ODE}~\eqref{eq:pendulum_ode}.
The same physical system can also be formulated in Cartesian coordinates $(r_x,\,r_y)$ for the bob position, related to the polar angle by $r_x = L\sin\theta$ and $r_y = -L\cos\theta$.
In this representation, the rigid rod introduces the geometric constraint that the bob must remain on a circle of radius $L$.

Applying Newton's second law in the Cartesian directions and enforcing the rod rigidity yields \cite{Modelica_Fritzson}
\begin{align}
    m\dot{v}_x &= -\frac{r_x}{L}\,F,              \label{eq:pendulum_dae_x}\\
    m\dot{v}_y &= -\frac{r_y}{L}\,F - mg,         \label{eq:pendulum_dae_y}\\
    \dot{r}_x  &= v_x,                            \label{eq:pendulum_dae_vx}\\
    \dot{r}_y  &= v_y,                            \label{eq:pendulum_dae_vy}\\
    0          &= r_x^2 + r_y^2 - L^2.           \label{eq:pendulum_constraint}
\end{align}
Here, $(r_x,r_y,v_x,v_y)$ are the position and velocity components and $F$ is the scalar constraint force in the rod.
Equations~\eqref{eq:pendulum_dae_x}--\eqref{eq:pendulum_dae_vy} are differential equations, whereas \eqref{eq:pendulum_constraint} is an algebraic constraint.
In the semi-explicit form introduced above, $(r_x,r_y,v_x,v_y)$ are the differential variables and $F$ is the algebraic variable.

The structural difficulty of a \ac{DAE} can be characterized by its \emph{differentiation index} which defines the minimum number of times the algebraic constraints must be differentiated with respect to time before the system can be reduced to an explicit \ac{ODE}~\cite{kunkel_differential-algebraic_2024}.
For the Cartesian pendulum, repeated differentiation of the length constraint~\eqref{eq:pendulum_constraint} reveals hidden constraints at each level:
%
\begin{align}
    \text{position:} \quad
        0 &= r_x^2 + r_y^2 - L^2,
        \label{eq:dae_index3} \\
    \text{velocity:} \quad
        0 &= r_x\,v_x + r_y\,v_y,
        \label{eq:dae_index2} \\
    \text{acceleration:} \quad
        0 &= v_x^2 + v_y^2
             - \frac{FL}{m} - g\,r_y,
        \label{eq:dae_index1} \\
    \text{ODE:} \quad
        \dot{F} &= -\frac{3mg}{L}\,v_y.
        \label{eq:dae_index0}
\end{align}
%
The velocity-level constraint~\eqref{eq:dae_index2} requires the velocity to be tangential to the circular path.
The acceleration-level constraint~\eqref{eq:dae_index1} expresses the centripetal acceleration required to maintain the circular motion, which depends on the constraint force $F$.
It also yields an explicit expression for the constraint force, $F = \tfrac{m}{L}(v_x^2 + v_y^2 - g\,r_y)$.
Three differentiations are required to obtain the \ac{ODE}~\eqref{eq:dae_index0} for the algebraic variable~$F$, so the Cartesian pendulum is a \ac{DAE} of differentiation index~3.

Equations~\eqref{eq:dae_index2} and~\eqref{eq:dae_index1} are \emph{hidden constraints} since they are not part of the original formulation but must be satisfied for the initial conditions to be consistent.
A numerical solver that operates only on the original index-3 system may experience constraint drift, since the position-level constraint~\eqref{eq:dae_index3} is not explicitly enforced during integration.
Modelica compilers address this by performing automatic index reduction, transforming the high-index \ac{DAE} into an equivalent lower-index system that standard \ac{DAE} solvers such as the \ac{DASSL} can handle~\cite{Modelica_Fritzson, petzold_description_1982}.

This formulation shows that the same physical system may appear either as an \ac{ODE} or as a \ac{DAE}, depending on the choice of variables and coordinate system.
The \ac{ODE} form in generalized coordinates eliminates the geometric constraint explicitly.
The Cartesian formulation retains the constraint and therefore leads naturally to a \ac{DAE}.
This distinction is important in physical modeling, where algebraic constraints often arise directly from the chosen representation.

In addition to such internal constraints, algebraic relations may also arise when subsystems are connected through their interfaces.
These connection-induced algebraic loops are introduced in the following subsection.

\subsection{Direct Feedthrough and Algebraic Loops}
\label{sec:213_direct_feedthrough}

Building on the input-output view introduced in Section~\ref{sec:211_dynamical_systems}, consider a subsystem represented by
\begin{equation}
    \dot{x} = f(x,u,t),
    \qquad
    y = h(x,u,t),
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the state vector, $u$ is the input vector, and $y$ is the output vector.
A subsystem has \emph{direct feedthrough} if the current output depends directly on the current input.
If the output depends only on the state, then no direct feedthrough is present.

In the linear time-invariant case,
\begin{equation}
    \dot{x} = Ax + Bu,
    \qquad
    y = Cx + Du,
\end{equation}
direct feedthrough is present if the matrix $D$ is nonzero \cite{astrom_feedback_2008}.
More generally, direct feedthrough is a structural property of the output relation $h(x,u,t)$.

This distinction becomes important when subsystems are connected through interface variables, that is, variables which are exchanged across subsystem boundaries.
Direct feedthrough alone does not imply an algebraic loop.
An \emph{algebraic loop} arises only when direct input-output dependencies form a closed cycle, so that the interface variables at the same time instant depend on one another.

A simple example is obtained by two interconnected subsystems
\begin{align}
    \dot{x}_A &= f_A(x_A,u_A,t),
    & y_A &= h_A(x_A,u_A,t), \\
    \dot{x}_B &= f_B(x_B,u_B,t),
    & y_B &= h_B(x_B,u_B,t),
\end{align}
with the coupling conditions
\begin{equation}
    u_A = y_B,
    \qquad
    u_B = y_A.
\end{equation}
The structure of this mutual interconnection is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:algebraic_loop}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/2_theoretical_background/algebraic_loop.pdf}
    \caption[Connected subsystems]{Two mutually connected subsystems. If both output relations have direct feedthrough, the interface variables form an algebraic loop.}
    \label{fig:algebraic_loop}
\end{figure}

If both $y_A$ and $y_B$ depend directly on the current inputs $u_A$ and $u_B$, then the coupling relations cannot be evaluated by simple sequential signal propagation.
Instead, the interface variables must satisfy a set of algebraic equations simultaneously.
The interconnection therefore introduces an algebraic loop.

If, in contrast, at least one subsystem output depends only on its internal state, the instantaneous circular dependency is broken and the coupled system may be evaluated sequentially.
Direct feedthrough is therefore an important structural property in interconnected continuous-time models.

Interface-induced algebraic relations are conceptually distinct from the internal algebraic constraints discussed in Section~\ref{sec:212_ode_dae}.
The Cartesian pendulum example contains an internal geometric constraint within a single model.
By contrast, direct feedthrough concerns algebraic dependencies that arise from the interconnection of subsystems through their inputs and outputs.

These structural properties, direct feedthrough and the resulting algebraic loops, are used in Section~\ref{sec:233_cosim_structural_analysis} to derive the execution order and to detect and resolve algebraic dependencies in coupled co-simulation systems.
