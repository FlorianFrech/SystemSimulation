\section{Continuous-Time System Modeling}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Dynamical Systems with Inputs and Outputs}
A model is a mathematical representation of a real system.
It is used to describe the system behavior and to answer questions by analysis or simulation.
The same physical system can be represented by different models.
The choice of model depends on the question of interest and on the required level of detail \cite{astrom_feedback_2008, karnopp_system_2012}.

A dynamical system is a system whose behavior changes over time.
In such systems, the effect of an action does not occur instantaneously.
Instead, the future behavior depends on the current condition of the system and on external influences.
Examples are mechanical motion, electrical circuits, and thermal processes \cite{astrom_feedback_2008}.

In engineering, complex systems are usually described as compositions of smaller subsystems or components.
Each component interacts with its environment through inputs and outputs.
Inputs represent external influences acting on the component.
Outputs represent variables that are provided to other components or to the environment.
This input-output view is useful because it supports the modular description of larger systems from connected subsystems \cite{karnopp_system_2012}.

In addition to inputs and outputs, a dynamical system is characterized by its state.
The state is a set of variables that contains the information required to predict the future evolution of the system for a given input.
The set of all possible states is called the state space.
For physical systems, state variables often represent quantities such as position, velocity, momentum, or stored energy \cite{astrom_feedback_2008,karnopp_system_2012}.

A common representation of continuous-time dynamical systems is the state-space form
\begin{equation}
    \dot{x} = f(x,u,t), \qquad y = h(x,u,t),
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the state vector, $u$ is the input vector, and $y$ is the output vector.
The function $f$ describes the time evolution of the state.
The function $h$ defines how the outputs are obtained from the state and the inputs.
If the functions do not depend explicitly on time, the system is called time-invariant \cite{astrom_feedback_2008}.

As a simple example, consider an RC circuit with input voltage $u(t)$ and capacitor voltage $v_C(t)$.
The capacitor voltage stores the past effect of the input and is therefore a natural state variable.
With
\[
x(t) = v_C(t),
\]
the system can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \dot{x} = -\frac{1}{RC}x + \frac{1}{RC}u,
    \qquad
    y = x.
\end{equation}
This is a continuous-time system with one state, one input, and one output.
Its future behavior depends on the current capacitor voltage and on the applied input voltage.

For linear time-invariant systems, the state-space form reduces to
\begin{equation}
    \dot{x} = Ax + Bu, \qquad y = Cx + Du,
\end{equation}
where $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ are constant matrices.
This representation is widely used in systems and control theory.
It also makes clear that the output may depend not only on the state, but also directly on the input through the matrix $D$.
This property is important in co-simulation, because it is closely related to the concept of direct feedthrough discussed later in this thesis \cite{astrom_feedback_2008}.

\subsection{Ordinary and Differential-Algebraic Equations}

Many continuous-time systems can be described by ordinary differential equations.
In this case, the time derivative of the state is given explicitly as a function of the current state, the input, and time \cite{kunkel_differential-algebraic_2024,css_cellier_kofman}.
A general nonlinear ODE can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \dot{x} = f(x,u,t),
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the state vector and $u$ is the input vector.

However, not all systems can be written naturally in explicit ODE form.
In many physical and component-based models, additional algebraic constraints are present.
These constraints may arise from constitutive relations, conservation laws, interface conditions, or the interconnection of subsystems \cite{css_cellier_kofman, karnopp_system_2012}.
In such cases, the model is more naturally written as a differential-algebraic equation.

A general DAE can be written in implicit form as
\begin{equation}
    F(t,z,\dot{z},u) = 0,
\end{equation}
where $z$ contains the model variables and $u$ denotes the input.
In contrast to an ODE, the derivative is not given explicitly.
Instead, differential and algebraic relations are stated together \cite{kunkel_differential-algebraic_2024}.

A common semi-explicit form of a DAE is
\begin{align}
    \dot{x} &= f(x,w,u,t), \\
    0 &= g(x,w,u,t),
\end{align}
where $x$ contains the differential states and $w$ contains algebraic variables.
The first equation describes the time evolution of the states.
The second equation imposes algebraic constraints that must hold at every time instant.
This form is useful because it separates dynamic behavior from purely algebraic relations \cite{kunkel_differential-algebraic_2024,css_cellier_kofman}.

In contrast to ODEs, a DAE does not in general admit arbitrary initial values.
The initial state must be consistent with the algebraic constraints.
In addition, DAEs are often classified by an index that reflects the structural difficulty of the problem.
Higher-index systems are generally harder to analyse and simulate \cite{kunkel_differential-algebraic_2024}.

DAEs are especially important in component-based and equation-based modelling.
When subsystems are connected, additional interface equations are introduced.
As a result, variables that appear to be independent in isolated subsystem models may become constrained in the coupled system.
For this reason, DAE formulations are often more suitable than pure ODE formulations for multi-domain physical systems \cite{css_cellier_kofman,karnopp_system_2012}.

This is particularly relevant for modern modeling and simulation workflows.
Subsystem models are often created separately and then coupled through algebraic constraints.
In such settings, it is usually preferable to treat the DAE directly rather than to transform it manually into an ODE \cite{kunkel_differential-algebraic_2024}.

This distinction is relevant for the present thesis.
Equation-based models exported as FMUs may contain direct dependencies and algebraic relations at their interfaces.
These relations influence execution ordering, direct feedthrough analysis, and the treatment of algebraic loops in co-simulation.
For this reason, ODE and DAE models form the mathematical basis for the system representations considered in \emph{SysSimX}.

\subsection{Direct Feedthrough and Interface Variables}

When a system is decomposed into subsystems, each subsystem interacts with its environment through interface variables.
These variables describe the quantities that cross the subsystem boundary.
In a general systems view, they are represented as inputs and outputs.
In physical systems, they may correspond to quantities such as force and velocity, voltage and current, or more abstract control and measurement signals \cite{karnopp_system_2012,astrom_feedback_2008}.

The input-output view is useful because it allows complex systems to be described as networks of connected subsystems.
Each subsystem can then be studied and implemented as a separate model, while the overall behavior is obtained from the interconnection of these models \cite{karnopp_system_2012,astrom_feedback_2008}.
This is a central idea in component-based modeling and simulation.

Consider a subsystem in state-space form
\begin{equation}
    \dot{x} = f(x,u,t), \qquad y = h(x,u,t),
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the state vector, $u$ is the input vector, and $y$ is the output vector.
A subsystem has direct feedthrough if the current output depends directly on the current input.
If the output depends only on the state, then no direct feedthrough is present.
In the linear time-invariant case,
\begin{equation}
    \dot{x} = Ax + Bu, \qquad y = Cx + Du,
\end{equation}
direct feedthrough is present if the matrix $D$ is nonzero \cite{astrom_feedback_2008}.

This distinction is important in interconnected systems.
If the output of one subsystem depends instantaneously on its input, and this output is connected to the input of another subsystem, a zero-delay dependency is created.
If such dependencies form a closed loop, the coupled system may no longer be representable as a pure ODE.
Instead, algebraic equations must be solved together with the differential equations, which leads naturally to a DAE or an algebraic loop \cite{astrom_feedback_2008,css_cellier_kofman,kunkel_differential-algebraic_2024}.

In component-based physical modeling, interface variables are therefore not only communication signals.
They also define how subsystem equations are coupled across their boundaries.
This is particularly relevant in equation-based and multi-domain models, where interface conditions may introduce algebraic constraints between connected subsystems \cite{css_cellier_kofman,karnopp_system_2012}.

For the present thesis, direct feedthrough is an important structural property of simulation components.
It determines whether current outputs can be evaluated independently of current inputs or whether instantaneous dependencies must be taken into account.
This distinction is used later to derive execution order, identify zero-delay dependencies, and detect algebraic loops in coupled co-simulation systems.
