# Golden Rules for Writing Research Papers
_Based on Hengl & Gould (2002), "Rules of Thumb for Writing Research Articles"_

## 1. Core idea

A good research article is not defined by complicated language. It is defined by:

- clear structure
- logical flow
- focused argumentation
- coherent writing
- clear distinction between your work and prior work
- results and conclusions that answer the stated research question

The paper should be readable, reproducible, and relevant.

---

## 2. Standard paper structure

A research article typically follows the **IMRaD** logic:

1. **Introduction**
2. **Methodology**
3. **Results**
4. **Discussion / Conclusions**

Supporting elements:

- Title
- Abstract
- Keywords
- References
- Optional appendices, acknowledgements, abbreviations

---

## 3. Section-by-section rules

## Title

**Function**
- Indicates the topic and main discovery
- Attracts the reader’s attention

**Rules**
- Keep it short and simple
- Aim for roughly **7–10 words**
- Make it specific and purposive
- Avoid vague phrasing and redundancy

**Avoid**
- “An investigation of …”
- “The analysis of …”
- “Effect of …”
- “Influence of …”

---

## Abstract

**Function**
- Tells the whole story in miniature
- Must work as a standalone summary

**Rules**
- Keep it concise
- Usually use **past tense**
- No citations, tables, equations, or figures
- State clearly:
  - what was done
  - what was found
  - what the main conclusions are
- Include key quantitative results where possible

**Practical rule**
- The abstract is one of the most important parts of the paper because many readers only read the abstract.

---

## Introduction

**Function**
- Introduces the topic
- Defines terminology
- Positions the work in the literature
- States the research gap and the objective

**Recommended move structure**
1. Introduce the topic and explain why it matters
2. Relate the work to current knowledge
3. Identify the gap
4. Introduce your work
5. State the research questions and objectives

**Rules**
- Use present tense for established knowledge
- Use past tense for literature review when appropriate
- Use up-to-date references
- Define terms clearly
- Make the gap explicit

---

## Methodology

**Function**
- Enables another competent researcher to repeat the work

**Rules**
- Answer: **who, what, when, where, how, and why**
- Use **past tense**
- Prefer **active voice**
- Be precise with units, variables, materials, and settings
- Include everything that matters for interpreting the results
- If a technique is standard, name it without re-explaining basic textbook theory
- Use simple examples to explain complex procedures

**Practical rule**
- Treat the methodology like a **cookbook**: specific, structured, reproducible.

---

## Results

**Function**
- Present the findings
- Compare treatments, cases, or configurations
- Provide quantitative evidence

**Rules**
- Use past tense
- Use figures and tables strategically
- Present results related to the research objective, not every intermediate output
- Emphasize the most important findings
- Separate your results clearly from literature comparison

**Do**
- Summarize findings in graphics and numbers
- Highlight comparisons
- Report quantitative support for claims

---

## Discussion / Conclusions

**Function**
- Answer the research questions
- Interpret the results
- Explain discrepancies and unexpected findings
- State the importance and limitations of the work
- Indicate further research

**Rules**
- Do not merely repeat the results
- Make statements
- Use strong formulations when justified
- Do not hide unexpected findings
- Discuss limitations openly
- Explain why the findings matter

**Better**
- “We concluded that …”

**Weaker**
- “It may be concluded that …”

---

## References

**Function**
- Document the knowledge base and sources used

**Rules**
- Cite accessible, relevant, and current sources
- Prefer **primary sources** over review sources when possible
- Follow the journal or thesis citation style consistently

---

## 4. The main “golden rules”

## Take a reader’s view
Write for your audience, not for yourself.

## Tell a story
A paper is not a dump of results. It needs a directed narrative.

## Be yourself
Write naturally first, then revise and polish.

## Make it simple
Use the simplest explanation that is still technically accurate.

## Make it concrete
Prefer concrete words and strong verbs. Avoid vague abstractions and overloaded noun clusters.

## Make it short
Avoid repetition, redundancy, and over-explaining familiar concepts.

## Take responsibility
Clearly distinguish between your contribution and previous work.

## Make strong statements
State your conclusions directly when your evidence supports them.

## Be self-critical
Acknowledge uncertainty, limitations, and the work of others.

---

## 5. What gets papers rejected

Common reasons for rejection mentioned in the source:

- irrelevant or too local topic
- lack of novelty
- poor connection between topic, objectives, and conclusions
- weak methodology
- unclear argumentation
- unfocused, incoherent writing
- poor data quality or insufficient evidence

---

## 6. Practical writing principles

## Prefer clarity over pseudo-scientific style
Simple, coherent writing is better than inflated language.

## Use examples
Examples improve understanding, especially for complex methodology.

## Maintain logical flow
Each section and subsection should perform a clear function.

## Keep structure visible
Use signposts and subsections where helpful.

## Focus on relevance
Only include material that supports the research question and paper objective.

## 7. Suggested subsection logic

## Introduction
- topic and definitions
- current state of knowledge
- gap
- objective / research questions

## Methodology
- techniques
- setup
- object of study / system / data
- assumptions

## Results
- main discoveries
- summary comparisons
- quantified evidence

## Discussion
- answers to research questions
- interpretation
- discrepancies / unexpected findings
- limitations
- importance / implications
- future work

## 8. Condensed checklist for drafting a paper

## Before writing
- Define the paper’s main message
- Formulate the research question
- Identify the target audience
- Draft a working title

## While writing the introduction
- Explain the topic
- Show why it matters
- Summarize relevant prior work
- Identify the gap
- State objective and scope

## While writing the methodology
- Make the work reproducible
- State all important assumptions and settings
- Use standard terminology and units
- Explain only what the reader needs

## While writing the results
- Present only relevant results
- Use figures and tables selectively
- Highlight the main findings
- Quantify claims

## While writing the discussion
- Answer the research question explicitly
- Interpret the results
- Discuss limitations
- Explain significance
- Identify future work

## Before submission
- Check whether title and abstract match the content
- Check whether the conclusions answer the stated objective
- Remove redundant statements
- Ensure figures, tables, and equations are referenced in the text
- Check consistency of terminology, abbreviations, and units
- Update and verify references

---

## 9. Condensed 22-step writing workflow

1. Make a working title  
2. Introduce the topic and define terminology  
3. Explain why the topic matters  
4. Relate to current knowledge  
5. Identify the gap  
6. Pose the research question  
7. State purpose and objectives  
8. List methodological steps  
9. Explain the methodological basis  
10. Describe the experimental or simulation setup  
11. Describe the object of study / system / data  
12. Present summary results  
13. Compare results  
14. Focus on the main discoveries  
15. Answer the research question  
16. Support and defend the answer  
17. Explain conflicting or unexpected findings  
18. State limitations  
19. State the importance of findings  
20. Establish newness  
21. Announce further research  
22. Write the abstract last as the miniature version of the paper

---

## 10. Best short takeaway

A strong paper is:

- clear
- coherent
- focused
- well structured
- evidence-based
- honest about limits
- written for the reader

And in practice:

> **Draft → revise → polish**
